Meta Raises $15 Billion in Dollar Bonds, Then Draws a Record €53 Billion of Orders for a €9 Billion Euro Debut

What's new since last edition: the bond sale that was only reported is now done. Meta sold bonds in two currencies to pay for AI infrastructure. Its first euro deal drew the most orders of any corporate bond sale in Europe, even though S&P had just cut its outlook to negative.

Read more

On Tuesday, November 3, Meta said it was raising $15 billion through a five-part US dollar bond sale, according to Reuters. CNBC had reported that the company was aiming for $20 billion to $25 billion in total, with about two-thirds in dollars and one-third in euros. On Wednesday, November 4, Bloomberg reported that Meta raised €9 billion in its first euro bond sale. That deal drew about €53 billion of orders, the most for any corporate deal in Europe. The previous record was more than €31 billion for Anheuser-Busch InBev in 2016. Bloomberg says the yields were generous enough to attract investors who are wary of the AI boom. The sale came days after S&P Global Ratings changed Meta's outlook from AA- stable to AA- negative, citing uncertainty over capital spending and negative free cash flow. Meta raised $25 billion in bonds in April. It said last week that it plans to raise $95 billion to fund its AI work.

Impact on the tech industry: Oracle's raise, covered last edition, showed the strain of paying for AI. Meta's record order book shows that investors will still lend to strong borrowers if the price is right. The cost of that money is going up, though, and the negative outlook is a warning. Euro investors are now a second source of funding for hyperscalers, which matters to Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and neoclouds planning their own debt sales. Watch how these bonds trade after issue, whether S&P or other agencies take further rating actions, and which hyperscaler sells bonds next.

Oracle Drops a $7 Billion Deal to Lease AI Chips to Tencent After Pressure From US Officials (Reported)

The five-year contract would have given Tencent access to about 100,000 advanced chips through Oracle data centers in Southeast Asia. No law blocked the deal. Officials in Washington, and separately in Guangdong, pushed against it.

Read more

AI Weekly, citing the Financial Times, reported on November 4 that Oracle has walked away from a five-year, $7 billion contract. The deal would have leased Tencent about 100,000 advanced AI chips through Oracle's data centers in Southeast Asia. Those chips cannot be sold into China because of restrictions from both Washington and Beijing. According to the report, Trump administration officials contacted Oracle to raise concerns. Separately, officials in Guangdong pressed Tencent to use Chinese-made chips. The FT says leasing AI chips overseas is still allowed under current rules. Political pressure ended this deal, not a new regulation. The decision comes days after Oracle filed to raise $20 billion in stock and bonds, as covered last edition, which makes losing a multibillion-dollar contract more noticeable.

Impact on the tech industry: Renting compute in third countries has been the main legal way for Chinese companies to use top US chips. This case shows Washington can shut that route through pressure without a formal rule. That is a risk for every US cloud provider in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, and for the neoclouds that serve Chinese customers. It also pushes Tencent further toward Chinese chips. Watch whether other providers cancel deals quietly and whether Commerce turns the pressure into formal rules.

Binance published its eighth recovery update on November 5. It says about 98% of the user asset value affected by the October 29 hack has been replaced, DailyCoin reports. Withdrawals have been restored for 62 tokens. Proof-of-reserves coverage has grown from 10 tokens to 44 and now uses zero-knowledge verification, which lets users confirm that their balances are backed at least one-for-one. FinanceFeeds says blockchain analysts have frozen about $2.8 billion of the stolen funds. It also points to conflicting reports on whether withdrawals are fully open again.

Impact on the tech industry: Binance appears to have survived the largest hack in crypto history without customers losing funds. Paying for the losses from its own balance sheet sets a high bar for other exchanges. Zero-knowledge proof of reserves may become the expected standard.
